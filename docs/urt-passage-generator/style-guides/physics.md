## Passage shape

- Length: 200–500 words (typical 310)
- Reading level: US grade 8.5–11 (typical 9.5)
- Questions per passage: 4–7 (typical 5), 4 options each
- Figures: 85% of passages, at most 3 per passage

## Styles

### Experiment report (`experiment_report`, 42%)

A lab-style account of controlled experiments. Each experiment varies one quantity and records the result in a table; later experiments test a different variable. The text explains what was measured and how, but leaves interpretation to the questions.

Structure: Short introduction of the phenomenon and the quantity being investigated → Setup or model (often a proposed equation with unknown constants or exponents) → One to three experiments, each changing one variable, each with its own data table → No conclusion paragraph: the questions draw the conclusions

### Expository with data (`expository`, 33%)

A textbook-like explanation of a concept (sound, refraction, phase changes, terminal velocity), sometimes framed by a real event, followed by graphs or a reference table that the questions use. Mixes conceptual questions answerable from the text with graph-reading questions.

Structure: Definition paragraph introducing the concept and its terms → Paragraph relating the measurable quantities (often with one formula) → One or two graphs or a reference table extending the text

### Data report (`data_report`, 17%)

Centred on one or two graphs (heating curves, spectra) with labelled points or regions. Most questions are direct readings or comparisons between the two figures.

Structure: Brief context and definitions → Description of a measurement over time or across conditions → Figure 1 (and often a comparable Figure 2 for a second substance or instrument)

### Comparison of two viewpoints (`two_viewpoints`, 8%)

Two scientists explain the same phenomenon differently. No figure is needed. Questions ask which viewpoint explains or is contradicted by an observation, and where the two agree.

Structure: One-sentence introduction of the phenomenon → Scientist 1: an older or mechanical explanation → Scientist 2: the modern explanation

## Difficulty levels

### Easy (36%)

The answer to most questions is stated in the text or read directly from one table row or one point on a graph. One reasoning step at most; no calculation beyond reading a value.

Passage markers:
- Clear definitions given before they are used
- Standard, clean graphs (e.g. heating curves with obvious plateaus) or small tables with monotonic trends
- One experiment or one figure carries most of the questions

Question markers:
- Read a value or identify a labelled point or region
- Identify which variable was changed in an experiment
- Rank items directly from a table column

### Medium (64%)

Questions need two or three steps: combining the text with a figure, comparing trends across two series or tables, interpolating, or a short proportional calculation. Most URT physics passages are at this level.

Passage markers:
- Two or more experiments or figures that must be related to each other
- A formula with unknown exponents or constants to be found from data
- Graphs with two series (e.g. two conditions) or non-trivial axes (unit conversion, reversed axis)

Question markers:
- Choose the pair of trials that isolates one variable
- Proportional scaling or ratio reasoning to find an unknown value
- Compare slopes or curvature between series
- Interpolate between tabulated values

### Hard (0%)

Not used as an overall passage level: no passage in the corpus was hard. Individual hard questions (about 6%) appear inside medium passages: they chain several steps, such as deriving an exponent from ratios and then predicting a graph shape, or applying an outside concept to the data.

Passage markers:
- Not used at passage level

Question markers:
- Multi-step derivation from data followed by a prediction
- Requires a physics principle not stated in the passage, applied to the figure

## Figures

### Table (50%): 3–8 rows × 2–6 columns

- Titled 'Table 1', 'Table 2', … and referred to by that name in the text and questions
- One row per trial or condition; first column identifies the trial, material or condition
- Column headers carry the quantity and its unit in parentheses, e.g. 'Velocity (m/s)'
- Values have consistent decimal places; trends are physically plausible and mostly monotonic
- Sometimes one cell is unknown (marked X) for the student to calculate

### Line chart (50%): 1–2 series × 5–12 points

- Titled 'Figure 1', 'Figure 2', …; axes labelled with quantity and unit
- Two series compare two conditions (e.g. dry vs humid air) on the same axes
- Heating-curve style graphs may label points or segments with letters (A–E) that questions refer to
- A pair of figures for two substances or instruments uses identical axes so they can be compared

## Question types

| Type | Share | Uses figure | Skills |
|---|---|---|---|
| Data interpretation (`data_interpretation`) | 41% | yes | Read a value or locate a labelled point on a graph; Compare two series or two figures; Match a relationship in the data to the shape of a graph |
| Inference (`inference`) | 25% | no | Relate physical quantities to observable effects (e.g. amplitude to loudness); Predict the outcome of a described situation using the passage's principle |
| Detail (`detail`) | 14% | no | Identify a condition or fact stated in the text; Identify which viewpoint explains a phenomenon |
| Calculation (`calculation`) | 8% | yes | Proportional scaling to find an unknown value; Interpolate between tabulated values; Net change across conditions |
| Hypothesis or viewpoint evaluation (`hypothesis_evaluation`) | 7% | no | Decide which viewpoint an observation contradicts; Choose the trials that test a stated hypothesis |
| Experimental design (`experimental_design`) | 5% | yes | Identify the independent variable or the purpose of a change; Select the minimum set of trials needed to determine an unknown |

- **Data interpretation:** Answered from a table or graph; the most common type.
- **Inference:** Goes one step beyond what is stated, using the passage's principle; sometimes uses the figure.
- **Detail:** Answer stated in the text; usually easy.
- **Calculation:** Short arithmetic from table or graph values; no calculus, simple ratios.
- **Hypothesis or viewpoint evaluation:** Tests a claim against the data or an observation.
- **Experimental design:** About controlled variables and what each experiment can show.

### Question stems

- Short, direct stems; figure questions name the figure ('According to Figure 1', 'Based on Table 2')
- Comparative stems ask how one condition differs from another
- Each passage carries a short title naming its topic (e.g. 'Passage I: <topic>')

## Distractors

- Wrong options reverse a comparison or a trend (greater vs smaller, increases vs decreases)
- Binary outcomes are paired symmetrically across options (e.g. a 2×2 grid of greater/smaller × longer/shorter)
- Data distractors use an adjacent row, column or the wrong series of the figure
- Trial-pair distractors pick pairs in which more than one variable changes
- Calculation distractors come from a common slip: linear instead of quadratic scaling, ignoring one factor
- Graph-shape options show the standard alternatives: linear, inverse, quadratic, decreasing
- Misconception distractors confuse related ideas (pitch vs speed, temperature rise vs phase-change plateau)

## Answer key

- Exactly four options, A–D, one correct
- Numeric options in ascending order, with units
- Pair options written as 'x and y', listed in numerical order
- Options are parallel in grammar and length
- Approximate values marked with '≈' or 'approximately'
- Correct-answer positions are balanced across A–D.

## Never do this

- Never require a figure that cannot be built from data (apparatus sketches, ray diagrams, photos); describe the setup in words instead
- No calculus and no multi-line derivations; arithmetic stays at ratios, scaling and interpolation
- No question answerable without reading the passage or its figure, except a minority (about one in five) testing a basic physics principle
- No 'all of the above' or 'none of the above' options (not seen in the corpus)
- No data that contradicts physics (e.g. a phase-change plateau that is not flat, sound slower in steel than in air)
- No two options that are both defensible; distractors must be clearly wrong on the data
- No conclusion paragraph that gives away the answers
